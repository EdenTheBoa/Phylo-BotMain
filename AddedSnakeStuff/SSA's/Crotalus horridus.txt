Timber rattlesnakes *Crotalus horridus* are large (insert average/record here) venomous rattlesnakes in the family Viperidae. This species inhabits a wide range across the eastern United States, reaching north to Minnesota and New Hampshire, and south to Texas and Northern Florida. These snakes range in coloration from very light brown to nearly black, but most color variations exhibit a black chevron pattern down their backs that makes them fairly easy to identify. Like all rattlesnakes, timber rattlesnakes posses a rattle at the end of their tail for use as a warning to potential predators. As their name would imply, these snakes prefer forest habitats with rocky ground, although they are found in lowlands and swamps throughout some portions of their range. Their primary prey consists of rodents, birds, and amphibians, but they will eat many other animals as well - including other snakes. Primarily ambush predators, timber rattlesnakes are generally lethargic and slow-moving unless they are disturbed or actively hunting. 

Historically, timber rattlesnakes and "canebrake rattlesnakes" were believed to be two separate subspecies, but recent genetic analysis has shown the two populations to be near-identical genetically. Thus, "timber rattlesnake" is the correct term for all populations of this species. Despite carrying an overall IUCN rating of "least concern" as a species, timber rattlesnakes have been extirpated from much of their historic range and are now listed as threatened or endangered in many states.

Timber rattlesnakes possess a potent venom delivered through hollow fangs at the front of the mouth. Like many other rattlesnakes, the exact venom composition varies among different populations. In general, southern populations have higher concentrations of neurotoxins, and northern populations have higher hemotoxic and proteolytic potentials. A bite from any timber rattlesnake is a medical emergency and should be treated as such. Treatment typically consists of supportive care and the use of CroFab antivenom as needed. Although timber rattlesnake bites have resulted in numerous fatalities over the past several decades, prognosis is usually good for healthy adults who seek prompt medical attention.
