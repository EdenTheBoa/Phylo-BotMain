Black Swampsnakes *Seminatrix pygaea* are small (25-38cm, up to 56cm) New World natricine snakes that range from eastern North Carolina south along the coastal plain into Georgia, extreme southern Alabama, and through Florida, from near sea level up to 150m.  They inhabit calm, well vegetated, freshwater habitat, including ponds, lakes, swamps, marshes, sluggish streams, and ditches.  Brackish wetlands are occasionally utilized as well.

Secretive in habit, *S. pygaea* are rarely observed even in areas where they are common.  They are active day and night, foraging amongst aquatic vegetation.  The prey primarily upon aquatic salamanders and salamander larvae, but other amphibians, small fish, and leeches are also consumed.

[Black Swampsnakes are black or dark brown above and bright red bellow](https://www.flickr.com/photos/tamers1/14750018926/).  Their dorsal scales are smooth and arranged in 17 rows across midbody.  Light colored, longitudinal lines down the center of the scales might resemble keels, especially along the lower scale rows.  They are unlikely to be confused with other snakes, but the superficially similar Red-Bellied Snakes *Storeria occipitomaculata* have strongly keeled dorsal scales which are arranged in 15 rows across the body, a light spot just behind the head, and a distinctively short, blunt snout.

For a ten year period from 2013-2023 this species was grouped as Liodytes until our understanding of the internal relationships of the swampsnakes improved and was revised, with this summary and associated link: "L. pygaea exhibits greater phenotypic and ecological differences with the other Liodytes, including paired internasals and a diet consisting mainly of small fish, amphibians, and earthworms (Gibbons and Dorcas, 2004) and was placed in the monotypic genus Seminatrix by Cope (1895) up until 2013. Given [our phylogenetic results](https://snakeevolution.org/pdfs/Nunez2023.pdf) and the notable phenotypic and ecological differences between the species, we recommend that L. pygaea be restored as Seminatrix pygaea."
