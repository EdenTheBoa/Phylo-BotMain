Giant Gartersnakes *Thamnophis gigas* are medium-large (91-122cm, record 163cm) New World natricine snakes endemic to the Central Valley of California, from Glenn and Butte Counties south to San Joaquin and Contra Costa Counties, and from Merced County south to Fresno County, from near sea level to 120m.  Historically they ranged throughout the Sacramento and San Joaquin drainages, from Butte County south to Kern County, but intense anthropogenic landscape alteration has eliminated an estimated 95% of their former habitat, especially in the southern portion of their range.  Their range is now very fragmented, with populations persisting in pockets of habitat that remain.  They mainly inhabit slow moving, heavily vegetated, and muddy bottomed wetlands and waterbodies, including marshes, sloughs, ditches, sluggish streams, and flooded agricultural areas.    They are protected as a Threatened Species both federally and by the state of California.

When cornered/frightened *T. gigas*, like many Gartersnakes and related Watersnakes, may flatten the head and body to make itself appear larger, bite or pretend to bite, and release a foul smelling musk from the vent. Mild toxins in the saliva might be effective in subduing smaller prey items, but bites are considered harmless to humans.

Primarily diurnal in habit, *T. gigas* can become nocturnal or crepuscular during periods of hot weather.  They are strongly semiaquatic and normally found in or near water.  Notoriously wary, individuals can be difficult to approach; those basking on matted grass or low vegetation will reportedly drop into the water when a human or other predator is spotted nearly 50 feet away.  Their diet consists of fish and amphibians, which are often ambushed underwater.

Giant Gartersnakes have a moderately sized head with an elongate muzzle and narrow snout.  The dorsal scales are strongly keeled and arranged in 21-23 rows anteriorly, 21-23 rows at midbody, and usually 17 (17-19) rows posteriorly.  There are usually eight (rarely nine) supralabial scales, with the seventh noticeably larger than the sixth, and 10-11 infralabials.  The internasal scales are longer than they are wide and distinctively shorter than the prefrontals.  A loreal scale is present in between the nasal and preocular scales.  The anal plate is undivided.  

The ground color ranges from olive brown to greenish grey, grey-brown, or black.  Three light colored stripes may be prominent, indistinct, or absent altogether and range in color from pale to dark yellow, tan, or orange brown, and from dull to bright in intensity.  Two rows of dark, alternating checkerboard spots may likewise be prominent, indistinct, or absent.

Other snakes which overlap in range may be confused for *T. gigas*.  Common Gartersnakes *T. sirtalis* have 19 dorsal scale rows anteriorly and at midbody, a differently shaped head with a shorter muzzle and broader snout, internasals which are wide and blunt anteriorly, and where they overlap with *T. gigas*, distinct red spots or bars above the lateral stripes.  Western Terrestrial Gartersnakes *T. elegans* have a differently shaped head with a shorter muzzle and broader snout, more laterally positioned eyes, the sixth supralabial is usually noticeably larger than the seventh, and the internasals are wide and blunt anteriorly.  Aquatic Gartersnakes *T. atratus* rarely occur along the edges of the Valley floor, and in these areas they typically have 19 dorsal scale rows anteriorly and at midbody and internasals as long or longer than the prefrontal scales.  Sierra Gartersnakes *T. couchii* also rarely occur along the edges of the Valley floor but their sixth supralabials are noticeably longer than the seventh and their internasal scales are noticeably longer than the prefrontals.  *Masticophis* whipsnakes typically occur in drier habitat, have smooth dorsal scales arranged in a maximum of 17 rows, and never have a light colored middorsal stripe.
